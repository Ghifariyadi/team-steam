01 · Research question

Why look at Steam reviews?

Steam reviews are a public record of how multiplayer games feel to play: players use them to report harassment, cheating and toxic communities. Irony can flip what a review says, so we ask how toxicity and irony show up in 7,365 reviews of Counter-Strike 2, PUBG and GTA V, and whether the toxic ones recommend the game less.

Research questionHow do literal, mixed and ironic evaluations intersect with gender stereotypes and accounts of harassment, and do reviews that describe toxic communities recommend games less?

  1. Players use game reviews to report harassment, cheating and toxic communities, so reviews are a public record of how multiplayer spaces feel to the people in them.
  2. Irony and sarcasm can reverse what a review says. A “10/10” can be a complaint, so a recommendation and its text do not always agree.
  3. Gender and age references show up in reviews as stereotypes, exclusion and self-description. They are what players say about themselves, and nobody verifies them.
  4. Toxicity mention (harm_mention). The review reports, condemns, endorses or expresses insults, threats, discrimination, deliberate disruption or a toxic community. A cheating complaint counts only when it also has one of these. Bugs, pricing complaints and profanity alone do not count.
  5. Irony (irony). A supported ironic reading, including sarcasm: the literal evaluation contrasts with the intended one, or a described outcome mismatches the expectation. Humor or exaggeration alone does not count.

02 · Data

7,365 reviews, three games.

About 80% of the reviews are not in English. Each review stays in its original language, and labels come from the original text, never from a translation.

  1. Source. KieranPoc’s Kaggle Steam reviews dataset (weighted_score_above_08.csv), collected through the Steam reviews API and loaded with kagglehub. The file is already filtered to reviews with a weighted score above 0.8, so it is a subset and not every review. Each row is one review. We keep three games: Counter-Strike 2 (3,017), PUBG: BATTLEGROUNDS (2,404) and Grand Theft Auto V (1,944), 7,365 reviews in total.
  2. Preparation. Empty reviews are skipped (2 of them) instead of getting an invented label, which leaves 7,363 labeled reviews. For readers, non-English reviews are also translated into English in a separate column (translation_en), and that translation is never sent for labeling. We label from the original text because emotion is easier to catch before translation, since machine translation tends to lose or flatten it (Kabir et al., 2025).

03 · Method

Three passes, plus a human pilot.

Gemini labels each review from a written codebook. The toxic ones get a second check with one added rule, that cheating alone is not toxic, and a third pass that quotes the toxic words. Two humans labeled a 90-review pilot to test the codebook.

  1. First pass: labeling. gemini-3.5-flash-lite through Vertex AI at temperature 0. The project codebook is the system prompt, and each review goes in its own request with its game and its Recommended / Not recommended flag. The answer is structured JSON: harm_mention, irony and gender_age, each with a short English reason. The run uses 8 parallel requests, retries on temporary Google errors, and saves every finished label right away, so a re-run skips what is already done. It took 46 minutes.
  2. Second pass: the cheating rule. The 3,499 reviews labeled toxic in the first pass go back to Gemini with the full codebook plus one added rule, that a cheating complaint alone is not toxicity. Reviews first labeled no are not re-asked, since a narrower rule can only turn a yes into a no. The first-pass labels are kept unchanged and the second pass is saved in separate files.
  3. Third pass: the toxic words. On the 1,669 reviews still toxic, Gemini quotes up to 3 exact words or phrases that make the review toxic, and gives each a type (insult, identity attack, threat, swearing, sexual harassment, griefing or trolling, toxic community, other), the identity group if it is an identity attack, and who it is aimed at. The types follow common schemes: the Perspective API attributes, Risch and Krestel (2020), and the Fair Play Alliance and ADL framework for online games (2020). A quote counts only if it appears word for word in the review: 96.2% did, and the rest are left out. 1,620 reviews got at least one quote; 49 got none (7 requests failed and the others returned no quote). This pass is saved in separate files too.
  4. Human pilot. 90 reviews (30 per game), labeled blind by two annotators, with about 10% positive cases. Only 1 of the 90 is not in English. We measured Cohen’s kappa between the two annotators to see whether the codebook can be applied the same way by different people.
  5. Trial and next check. A 10-review trial gave Gemini only reviews where a human had marked toxicity or irony as yes, then compared its labels with the human ones. The next check is a stratified sample of non-English reviews, labeled by a human who reads the language, to test Gemini outside English.
  6. Ethics. User IDs are left out and only short public review text is shown as examples. Identity claims in reviews are unverified. A yes label means the text says it, not that the reviewer did it or that the event happened.

04 · Findings

What Gemini’s labels show.

Gemini flagged toxicity in 22.7% of the 7,363 labeled reviews and irony in 25.0%. Counter-Strike 2 has the most of both. Most toxic reviews insult other players, and reviews that do not recommend the game are toxic more often.

  1. Gemini labeled every review. 7,363 of the 7,365 reviews got a label with no failures. The other 2 were empty and skipped. The run took 46 minutes.
  2. Gemini flagged toxicity in 22.7% of the reviews and irony in 25.0% (1,669 and 1,843 of 7,363). Our first pass flagged toxicity in 47.5% (3,499 reviews), but most of those only complained about cheaters or hackers. We then added one rule to the codebook, that cheating alone is not toxic, and Gemini re-checked those 3,499 reviews. Cheating counts only when the review also has insults, slurs, nationality stereotypes, threats, harassment or deliberate disruption. The re-check turned 1,829 of the 3,499 to no and left 1,669 as yes, and 1 as uncertain. About 1,670 of those changes were reviews that mention cheating. The other 161 were reviews without cheating that Gemini no longer counted as toxic on the second look, often single-word or joke reviews. Irony was not re-checked. Uncertain answers were rare in the first pass: 12 for toxicity and 96 for irony.
  3. Counter-Strike 2 has the most irony. 32.9% of its reviews are labeled ironic, against about 19% for GTA V and PUBG. On toxicity with the cheating rule, Counter-Strike 2 is highest (27.9%), then PUBG (20.8%) and GTA V (16.8%). The first pass put PUBG and Counter-Strike 2 level near 50%, so the cheating rule moved PUBG the most.
  4. Reviews that do not recommend the game are flagged for toxicity more often: 28.4% of the 2,369 “Not recommended” reviews, against 19.9% of the 4,994 “Recommended” ones. In the first pass the gap was larger (65.3% against 39.1%). This is an association between Gemini’s labels and the recommendation flag, not evidence of cause.
  5. These labels are Gemini’s, and they are not checked against humans on this data yet. The human pilot reviews are much shorter (median 28 characters, against 137 in the full data) and only 9 of the 90 appear in the 7,365, so the pilot’s 10% toxicity rate cannot be compared with the full-run rate.
  6. Human agreement on the pilot is high. Toxicity kappa is 0.845 and irony kappa is 0.903. This was measured before the cheating rule was added.
  7. Gemini matched the human label on 9 of 10 trial reviews for toxicity and on 10 of 10 for irony. The trial reviews were all human-positive and the codebook was tuned on the same cases, so this is a best case and not an accuracy estimate.

05 · Interpretation

How you define “toxic” changes the answer.

A written codebook lets an LLM label thousands of reviews and give a reason for each, so the labels can be audited one by one. But how we define toxicity changes the answer more than the model does: one extra rule moved the rate from 47.5% to 22.7%. Reviews that do not recommend a game are toxic more often, which is an association, not a cause.

What the results support

  1. Conclusion. A written codebook with structured output gives every label a reason, so the labels can be checked one by one. Finished labels are saved as they arrive, so the full run can be paused, resumed and re-checked without paying twice.
  2. Definitions matter. One extra rule (cheating alone is not toxic) cut the toxicity rate from 47.5% to 22.7%, so the definition changes the result more than the choice of model.
  3. Toxic communities and recommendations. Reviews that do not recommend the game are toxic more often (28.4% against 19.9%). Reviewers who dislike a game may also be more likely to complain about other players, so this is a link and not a cause.

What they do not support

  1. Accuracy outside English. That Gemini is accurate on non-English reviews. That has not been tested yet.
  2. That 22.7% of Steam reviews mention toxicity. That is Gemini’s rate on these three games, and it has not been validated. It depends on the cheating rule: the first pass without it gave 47.5%.
  3. How common toxicity, irony or gender talk is on Steam in general.
  4. That toxic communities cause fewer recommendations. Any link between labels and recommendations is an association.

06 · Limitations

What this does not show.

  1. The pilot is small (90 reviews, about 10% positive), so the kappa values are rough.
  2. The codebook was checked almost only on English reviews, but about 80% of the data is not English.
  3. The Gemini trial used only human-positive reviews and the codebook was tuned on the same cases.
  4. The quoted phrases and their types are Gemini’s own explanation, not proof of why it decided, and we have not yet checked them against a human (a 30-row spot-check file is ready). 185 reviews (11%) have toxic words aimed only at the developer, which the cheating rule says is not enough on its own, so some of them are probably false positives.
  5. The cheating rule was written after we saw the first-pass results, to fit how we want to define toxicity. It changes what the toxicity label means, and we have not checked it against humans.
  6. The full-run labels come from Gemini alone. We have not yet compared them with humans on a random sample of these reviews.
  7. Labels describe what the text says. They do not verify behavior, identity or events.
  8. Three games only, from one pre-filtered Kaggle file, so the results do not cover Steam as a whole.

07 · Recommendation for Steam

What Steam could do next.

We know game communities tend to be toxic. But racism and insulting someone’s identity cannot be justified. Here are four patterns from our data that Steam could turn into guidelines, with the reason and the indicators behind each label.

  1. Insults aimed at other players (48.5% of the toxic reviews).
    Suggested guideline. Criticize the play, not the person. Name-calling aimed at a player should be removable.
    Why we labeled it toxic. Words that attack a person’s skill, intelligence or character, like idiot, trash or worse. The target is a person. In 71% of these reviews Gemini marked players in general, and in 10% a teammate.
    Example. “First up are the trolls. A bunch of no-life cunts whom nobody likes…” (Polish, translated).
  2. Attacks on someone’s identity (25.9% of the toxic reviews).
    Suggested guideline. No attacks on who someone is: nationality, ethnicity, race, gender, sexuality, religion, age or disability. That includes blaming a whole nationality for cheating.
    Why we labeled it toxic. A group word together with a slur, an insult or a blame. Complaining about cheating alone does not count, but blaming it on a nationality does. In 88% of these reviews the group is a nationality or ethnicity, and 81% are aimed at a nationality group.
    Example. “This game is so much fun when those motherfucking Russians aren’t around.” (Turkish, translated).
  3. Swearing (20.1% of the toxic reviews).
    Suggested guideline. Swearing alone should stay allowed, especially at the game or the developer. It becomes a problem when it is aimed at a person.
    Why we labeled it toxic. Obscene language that is not clearly aimed at a person or a group. This is the weakest pattern. 41% of these reviews swear at the developer, which our own rule says is not toxic on its own. 48% are aimed at players in general.
    Example. “*I shoot 10 year old teammate accidently* Me: sorry Kid: FUCKING TEAMKILLER” (English).
  4. Threats and wishing harm (6.2% of the toxic reviews).
    Suggested guideline. Threats and wishing someone dead or sick should be removable.
    Why we labeled it toxic. Words that say or wish harm on someone, like dying, cancer or a painful disease. The rarest of the four, and 60% of these reviews are aimed at players in general.
    Example. “…smurfs who come back in full force every time there’s a sale (may you die a slow and painful disease, guys)…” (French, translated).
✓What to leave alone: criticizing the game, bug and price complaints, and reports of cheaters on their own. Most “Not recommended” reviews (71.6%) were not flagged as toxic.

Shares are of the 1,669 toxic reviews, and a review can have several types. Gemini used eight types. The other four are smaller or are not a behavior a guideline can target: reviews that describe a toxic community (11.1%), other (7.8%), sexual harassment (1.0%) and griefing or trolling (0.8%). These are suggestions from our data. We did not test whether guidelines like these reduce toxicity, and the labels are not checked against humans yet. The shares of targets come from Gemini’s own “target” field.

08 · Reproduce

Run it yourself.

The code, data files and run instructions are in the GitHub repository, and the notebooks run in the order listed below.

  1. Repository. team-steam on GitHub
  2. Environment. Run uv sync --frozen from the repository folder.
  3. Run order. notebooks/kappa_test.ipynb (human agreement), then notebooks/scale_up.ipynb (10-review trial, then the full run; the plumbing is in scripts/steam_labeling.py), then notebooks/harm_recheck.ipynb (the cheating rule) and notebooks/toxic_evidence.ipynb (the toxic words).
  4. Data access. The dataset downloads through kagglehub. The Gemini calls need your own Google Cloud (Vertex AI) or Gemini API access, set in a local .env file that is not committed.
reproduce it
$ git clone https://github.com/Ghifariyadi/team-steam.git$ cd team-steam$ uv sync --frozen
