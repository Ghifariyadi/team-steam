Research question

Players use reviews to report harassment and toxic communities. Irony can flip what a review says. So how do toxicity and irony show up, and do toxic reviews recommend less?

Research questionHow do literal, mixed and ironic evaluations intersect with gender stereotypes and accounts of harassment, and do reviews that describe toxic communities recommend games less?

Method

A written codebook, Gemini, and two humans.

01 · CODEBOOK

The rulebook comes first.

It says exactly what counts as toxic and what counts as ironic, as yes / no rules.

✓Insults, slurs or threats aimed at peopletoxic
✓A “10/10” that is really a complaintironic
×Complaining about cheaters, on its ownnot toxic

04 · ONE RULE ADDED LATER

The cheating rule.

After the first results we added one rule to the codebook: complaining about cheaters alone is not toxic. Gemini then re-checked every review it had called toxic.

The rule came after we saw results, so it is a choice we made, not a blind test.

Interpretation

How you define “toxic” changes the answer.

✓

The definition mattered more than the model.

One extra rule, that cheating alone is not toxic, cut the toxicity rate roughly in half. Same AI, same reviews.

✓

Every label comes with a reason.

So we could audit them one by one, and found the cheating problem that way.

✓

Counting is not explaining.

We can say how many reviews mention toxicity. We cannot say what causes it.

Limitations

What this does not show.

!

Humans have not checked these labels yet.

The two annotators agreed with each other on 90 reviews. We have not compared Gemini with humans on the full 7,363.

!

About 80% of the reviews are not in English.

We tuned and tested the rulebook mostly on English. Gemini outside English is untested.

!

The cheating rule came after the first results.

So 22.7% is not a blind result. And it covers three games from one filtered file, not all of Steam.
